# Henkel Düsseldorf-Holthausen: energy use cases in €/ton and the first-visit request

All €/ton figures are euro per metric ton of finished product from the Düsseldorf site, per year, for 2025, with the euro per year next to them [D1, D5]. The ids in brackets point to `model.xlsx` (sheet `assumptions`) and to `DECISIONS.md`.

## 1. Situation read

Düsseldorf-Holthausen is one of Henkel's two largest sites. It makes detergents, cleaners and adhesives [R20]. The site gets its energy from Henkel's own CHP plant, Kraftwerk Holthausen, with 75 MW of electric capacity. The plant delivers steam and power to Henkel's own lines, to other companies on the site and to the grid [R48, R15]. Four of its five units are back-pressure steam turbines (Gegendruckturbinen) built between 1965 and 1983. Their power output follows the steam demand. Only the 8.6 MW gas turbine can run on its own [R42, R46]. In 2025 the plant burned about 1.05 million MWh of natural gas. I derived that from its verified 211 kt of CO2 [R50, R58]. Coal use ended in 2024, two small units were shut down in 2025, and since April 2026 waste heat from the site, with CHP heat as top-up, feeds a district-heating line of the Stadtwerke [R10, R47, R8]. The cost pressure is clear. In 2025 the plant had to buy 71 % of its CO2 allowances, at about 74 EUR per ton [R50, R52, R36]. Gas and CO2 together cost about 56 million EUR a year. That is 62 EUR per ton of Henkel's site output, or 42 to 120 EUR, since the site tonnage is not public and I had to estimate it as a range [R54, R73 to R75]. One fact would change this picture more than any other: How much of the plant's steam and power is for Henkel itself. Henkel's report says 169 kt of its Scope 1 emissions come from energy sold to other companies, and selling energy is what this plant does. If all of that comes from Holthausen, only a fifth of the plant's 211 kt were burned for Henkel's own products [R15, R50].

## 2. Ranking rule and shortlist

Before looking anything up, I wrote down what a good answer would look like: the metric above, a ranking rule with fixed anchors, and one evidence rule, that no number goes into the model without a source I opened myself. This is the first commit in the repository [D1 to D3]. The rule gives every use case 1 to 3 points on four questions. How well is it grounded in public facts about the site? How big is it, compared with the plant's energy cost of 56 million EUR? How much of it could one data export from the first visit quantify? And does it fit what rizm.de says the OS does, a decision the OS would run itself, or only a one-off project [D2]? A case Henkel has visibly already done is cut, or reduced to what is left. Ties go to the better grounded case, then to the case with fewer unproven assumptions [D2, D9]. Thirteen cases came out of the longlist. Three stayed [D10]. U1 is a 15-minute forecast of steam and power demand per consumer that drives the unit commitment of the CHP plant. It also includes the choice between waste heat and CHP heat for the district heating, which was a separate case before I merged it [D8]. U11 is boiler efficiency monitoring, only the monitoring part, since Henkel's own plan already covers efficiency projects [R10]. U2 is running the gas turbine on the day-ahead and intraday price spread. It won the third place against control reserve on the second tie-break [D9]. The other cases were cut for reasons you can read per case in the `usecases` sheet [D11]. For example, hedging and ETS purchase timing reduce risk but do not lower the expected bill, so they give no net saving. Load shifting has no known loads that could be shifted. The investment cases lose against CHP steam, which costs about 60 EUR per MWh, while an electrode boiler runs on power at 13 ct per kWh. After the calculation, the €/ton results kept the same order as the scores.

## 3. U1: forecast-driven unit commitment, 1.1 million EUR a year, 1.2 €/ton

When the plant knows the demand of every consumer fifteen minutes ahead, it can run its five units with fewer starts, less vented steam and a better load split. It can also move district-heat top-up from CHP heat to waste heat where the forecast allows it. The saving is κ × (fuel cost × plant fuel × saved share σ + district heat × moved share φ × cost difference) minus the operating effort. The inputs are 58 EUR per MWh for gas and CO2 [R59] and a saved fuel share of 1.5 % [R60]. The district heat is 30 000 MWh, derived from the 6 500 t of CO2 the city expects to save [R63], with 20 % of it moved [R64] at a cost difference of 60 EUR per MWh between CHP heat and waste heat [R67]. The operating effort is 0.2 million EUR per year [R68]. The result is 1.1 million EUR a year, or 1.2 €/ton, between 0.79 and 2.4 depending on the site tonnage. None of the three cases needs capital investment, the yearly operating effort is already deducted, so there is no payback period to show. σ and φ are my own estimates. The only benchmark I had for them was the model skeleton. If σ is 0.5 to 3 %, the result moves to 0.46 to 2.0 million EUR. If φ is 10 to 40 %, it moves to 0.89 to 1.4 million. With 2024 prices it is 1.0 million [R61, R62, R65, R66, R80]. κ is the share of the saving that stays with Henkel. I set it to 1, since Henkel buys the fuel and the allowances, and since the 56 million baseline uses the same boundary. If the contracts with the other companies pass the fuel cost through, Henkel keeps only a fifth, and U1 drops to 0.05 million EUR, or 0.06 €/ton [D13, R82, R83]. There is one place where I used a different number than the source. For the gas price I took Eurostat's lower consumption band on purpose. The plant's own volume points to the next band. Its price is 8 % higher and would lift every fuel saving by 6 %, without changing the ranking [R31]. The other differences to the sources have their reason in the register. Savings are valued at the full EUA price, since every ton of CO2 saved is one allowance less to buy [D12]. The OS would run this decision itself. The 15-minute unit commitment is exactly what its Operation Hub does [R1].

## 4. U11: boiler efficiency monitoring. U2: gas turbine dispatch

U11 is about detection time. A boiler that slowly drifts, through excess air, a hotter stack or scale on the tubes, burns more gas for the same steam. A digital twin that compares the expected fuel with the actual fuel sees the drift weeks before the next inspection would [R5]. In the OS this is a recurring analysis that a person acts on [R2]. The saving is the boiler fuel, 878 000 MWh after taking out the gas turbine's share [R58, R69, R70], times a recovered share of 1 % [R71], minus 0.1 million EUR of effort [R72]. That gives 0.41 million EUR a year, or 0.45 €/ton, between 0.30 and 0.91 depending on the tonnage. I put the 1 % at the low end of the 1 to 3 % the skeleton derived from two DOE tip sheets, which I opened myself. Henkel already runs an efficiency program, and a plant of this size almost certainly controls its excess air automatically, so that part of the saving is already taken. What remains for the monitoring to find is scale, fouling and a rising stack temperature [R71, S20, S21]. U2 is a sketch. The gas turbine is the one unit that can follow prices. One more run hour pays when the power is worth more than the gas and CO2 it burns, minus the heat its recovery boiler gives back. The break-even is 90 EUR per MWh [R59, R69, R84, R76]. What the power is worth depends on the hour. When the site imports, one avoided MWh is worth about 13 ct per kWh and the turbine always pays. When the site exports, only the hours with high day-ahead prices pay. How the turbine is run today is not public [R78, R28, R70]. The OS would run the turbine on its own, hour by hour [R1]. From the scoring, the order of magnitude is 0.26 to 0.8 million EUR a year, or 0.3 to 0.9 €/ton [usecases sheet, U2]. The three savings overlap, so they cannot simply be added. Running the gas turbine on prices (U2) is one of the decisions U1 already makes, and the boiler efficiencies that U11 measures are an input U1 uses when it splits the load.

## 5. First visit: data request and stakeholder

The data I would ask for is the plant's energy balance for 2025, split by customer: steam, power and heat delivered to Henkel's own lines, to BASF and KLK, to the Stadtwerke and to the grid, the fuel per unit, the type of contract per customer, and Henkel's site tonnage [D15]. This one request replaces the two numbers that every €/ton depends on, the site tonnage [R73] and the share of a saving that stays with Henkel [R82]. It also settles the boundary of the metric itself [D7]. The second-best request would be one year of 15-minute historian data on unit starts and vented steam. It would sharpen the main assumption of U1, but it can only be read once the balance shows which customer each steam flow serves. The person I would ask for is the one who runs the power plant as a unit, the utilities or power plant operations lead (Leiter Kraftwerk/Energieversorgung) [D16]. The energy balance, the contracts with the other companies and the dispatch rule of the units all sit with this role. One conversation covers the first request and most of the second.

## 6. What would change the result, and the tools used

Three things could change my answer. First, the contracts. If they pass the fuel cost through to the other companies, every fuel saving is worth only a fifth to Henkel, U11 no longer covers its own operating effort, and the ranking becomes U1 against U2, whose power side belongs to Henkel in every case [D13]. Second, the order of the first two cases. If both the saved fuel share and the moved heat share sit at their low end, U1 falls to 0.28 million EUR, below U11. The order is therefore not robust. The two rows that decide it, R60 and R71, are the first numbers I would replace with plant data [D14]. Third, the already-done rule. If the control system already runs O2 trim and a load allocation module, or if a direct marketer already dispatches the gas turbine, U11 and U2 shrink to almost nothing [R71, R70]. Price levels change the size of every saving and leave the order as it is [R31, R80]. About the tools. The model is a spreadsheet on purpose. A plant manager can check it without code, and every value cell reads its number from the register by id. I used Grammarly to refine some formulations and Claude in five numbered chats with a fixed preamble, plus one "red-team" pass. Each chat is in `AI_LOG.md` with what I kept and what I rejected, and every transcript is in `transcripts/`. The scores, every decision, the hand check and this write-up are my own work. My rule that nothing enters the register from a source I did not open caught three tool errors: a made-up sentence for the UBA emission factor, dead URLs from the source search, and DOE efficiency ranges attributed to sheets that do not contain them. The details are in the rejected column of the log [S19, S20, S22].
